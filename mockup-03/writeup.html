Background

This started as a weekend project to play with the Next.js App Router when it was still relatively new. The idea was simple: build a tool that generates lottery numbers, tracks historical drawings, and surfaces frequency patterns over time. Nothing groundbreaking, but a good excuse to learn the framework in a context more interesting than a todo app.

The initial version took about two days. Number generation, a basic frequency chart, and a responsive layout. I threw it up on Vercel and forgot about it for a few weeks.

What it did

The app supported multiple lottery game formats and let users explore historical data in a few ways:

  • Simulate random drawings across different game types (Powerball, Mega Millions, Pick 3, etc.)
  • View historical number frequency as heat maps and bar charts
  • Filter results by date range, number position, and game format
  • Save "favorite" number sets and track how they would have performed historically

The most popular feature turned out to be the historical "what-if" tracker. People would enter their usual numbers and see how many times they would have won over the past ten years.

Technical decisions

The data layer was intentionally simple. Historical drawing results were stored as flat JSON files, fetched at build time via getStaticProps, and revalidated on a schedule. No database, no API layer. For an app this size, the complexity wasn't justified.

The charting was handled with a lightweight canvas library rather than a full-featured charting framework. The goal was fast initial render and smooth interactions on mobile, and the smaller bundle helped with both.

Outcome

The app picked up modest organic traffic over a few months, mostly from people searching for lottery statistics. In early 2025, I was approached by a small gaming content company that wanted to integrate it into their existing platform. The acquisition was straightforward and the transition was clean.

Lessons learned

Small projects that solve a specific, searchable question tend to find an audience on their own. I didn't promote this anywhere. The traffic came entirely from people searching for lottery number frequency data. Building something that answers a real question, even a trivial one, is more effective than building something technically impressive that nobody is looking for.
